Every GCC tracks time to hire. Far fewer track what happens next: how long it takes a new joiner to become fully productive. Yet for a growing centre, that second number matters at least as much. A hire who takes most of a year to contribute fully is a significant cost, and a frustrating experience for everyone involved.

That figure varies by role and centre, but it points to a clear opportunity. Even modest reductions, repeated across every intake, add up to a substantial gain in capacity.

## Where the time goes

When centres look closely at why new hires take so long to ramp up, the reasons are rarely about technical skill. People are usually hired because they have the skills. What slows them down is everything around those skills.

**Missing context.** New joiners do not yet understand the business, the product, the history of decisions or who the key stakeholders are. Without that context, they cannot make good judgements.

**Slow access.** Laptops, system access, repositories and data permissions can take weeks to arrive. Every day without access is a day of limited contribution.

**Unclear expectations.** Many new joiners do not know what "good" looks like in their first few months, or what they are allowed to decide on their own.

**Mindset gaps.** Laterals from IT services firms often need time to adjust to ownership and outcome thinking in a captive centre.

**Weak manager support.** Busy managers with growing teams may not have time for regular check-ins, so new joiners struggle in silence.

### 1. Be ready on day one

Start provisioning before the joining date. A new hire who arrives to a working laptop, access to the right systems and a first task can contribute in days rather than weeks. It is the cheapest lever and often the most neglected.

### 2. Teach the business early

Give new joiners a structured introduction to the business they will support: what it sells, who its customers are, how it makes money and what the current priorities are. Domain context accelerates every technical decision that follows.

<Cta programme="services-to-captive-onboarding" />

### 3. Set clear first goals

Agree a simple 90-day plan in the first week, with specific milestones. It gives the new joiner direction and gives the manager a structure for check-ins.

### 4. Assign a buddy

A peer buddy, separate from the manager, gives new joiners someone to ask the questions they would not want to ask their manager. It speeds up the informal learning that documentation never covers.

---

### 5. Build a manager rhythm

Short weekly check-ins in the first three months make a large difference. They surface blockers early, reinforce expectations and help new joiners feel supported. Briefing hiring managers on what new joiners need, especially laterals from services firms, makes these conversations more useful.

### 6. Measure it

What gets measured gets improved. Define what "fully productive" means for each major role, for example handling a defined set of tasks independently, and track how long each intake takes to get there. Compare intakes over time to see whether changes are working.

## Making it a centre-wide habit

Reducing time to productivity is not a one-off project. It works best as a standard part of how the centre runs: a consistent onboarding programme for every intake, a manager briefing, a buddy system and a simple measure reported to leadership each quarter.

The benefits go beyond capacity. New joiners who become productive faster are more engaged, more likely to stay and more likely to recommend the centre to others. In a competitive talent market, that experience becomes part of the employer brand.

For L&D and HR leaders, time to productivity is also one of the clearest ways to show business impact from onboarding. It translates directly into a number business leaders understand.

---

## Different roles, different ramp-ups

Time to productivity is not one number. It varies widely by role, and a single target for the whole centre hides more than it reveals.

**Engineers** can often contribute code within weeks, but take much longer to understand the architecture, the history of design decisions and the business logic well enough to work independently. Pairing, architecture walkthroughs and early ownership of a small component help.

**Analysts and business-facing roles** depend heavily on domain knowledge. Someone joining a banking or insurance centre without industry experience may need structured domain upskilling before they can contribute fully.

**Managers** take the longest. They need to understand the team, the stakeholders at HQ, the unwritten rules of the organisation and their own decision rights. A lateral manager can look settled after a month and still be some way from full effectiveness.

**Support and operations roles** often have the clearest definition of "productive", such as handling a defined set of cases independently, which makes them a good place to start measuring.

Defining productivity by role makes the measure useful. It also shows where the biggest gains are. In many centres, improving onboarding for lateral managers and domain-heavy roles delivers more value than shaving days off engineering ramp-up.

### A note on remote and hybrid joiners

Hybrid working makes onboarding harder. New joiners who are rarely in the office miss the informal conversations where much of the learning happens. Centres with hybrid teams should be more deliberate: schedule in-person days for the first weeks, make buddy sessions explicit and record key walkthroughs so they can be revisited.
