Pune has quietly become one of India's most important locations for Global Capability Centres (GCCs). Engineering and manufacturing companies, banks, insurers and technology firms have all chosen the city, drawn by its talent, its institutions and its quality of life.

That concentration is good news for the city. For each individual centre, it creates a sharper challenge: when hundreds of GCCs are hiring from the same talent pool, how do you attract, keep and grow the people you need?

## Why Pune, and why now

Pune's appeal to GCCs is not new. The city has long been a centre for automotive and manufacturing engineering, which made it a natural home for engineering and R&D centres. Its universities and engineering colleges produce a steady stream of graduates, and its IT corridors, from Hinjewadi to Kharadi and Magarpatta, offer established infrastructure.

Proximity to Mumbai, a large experienced workforce and a quality of life many professionals prefer to larger metros have added to the pull. As more global organisations set up or expand centres, each new arrival makes the city more attractive to the next.

---

## What density means for talent

When many centres compete in one city, the talent market behaves differently.

**Experienced people have choices.** Senior engineers, architects and especially capable managers can move between centres easily, often without changing their commute. Counter-offers become routine.

**Salary competition has limits.** Most well-funded centres can match each other on pay. Once compensation is competitive, it stops being the reason people stay or leave.

**Middle management becomes the bottleneck.** Centres can usually hire individual contributors. Finding managers who can lead distributed teams, handle HQ stakeholders and develop their people is much harder, and those managers are the most heavily recruited.

## How centres in Pune can stand out

In a crowded market, the centres that win on talent tend to do a few things well.

### Build leaders rather than buying them

Hiring experienced managers from other centres is expensive and fragile; the next offer is always around the corner. Centres that identify high-potential individual contributors early and prepare them for leadership build loyalty along with capability. People rarely leave the organisation that invested in their first management role.

### Make first-line managers the retention strategy

The single biggest factor in whether someone stays is usually their manager. Investing in how first-line managers run one-to-ones, give feedback, delegate and advocate for their people pays off directly in retention.

### Offer ownership, not just tasks

Engineers in a city full of GCCs can find execution work anywhere. What is rarer is the chance to own a product, shape a roadmap or lead work that HQ depends on. Centres that win bigger mandates find it easier to attract ambitious people, and ambitious people help win bigger mandates.

### Onboard laterals well

Many Pune hires come from IT services firms, and many move between GCCs. A strong onboarding experience that helps them understand how your centre works with HQ reduces early attrition and shortens the time before they contribute.

## What this means for different roles in the centre

The talent pressures of a dense GCC city land differently on each part of the leadership team.

**For site leaders**, the priority is to make the centre's mission visible. People choose between centres partly on what the centre does and where it is heading. A site leader who can clearly explain the centre's mandate, its growth plans and the opportunities that creates gives recruiters and managers a story that salary bands cannot match.

**For HR and L&D heads**, the focus shifts from filling roles to building capability. A strong pipeline of internal candidates for management and specialist roles reduces dependence on the open market. Structured development for high-potential people, combined with onboarding that works for every intake, becomes a core part of the talent strategy rather than a support function.

**For engineering and CoE heads**, the challenge is to keep the work interesting. Engineers in Pune can see what other centres are building. Offering modern technology, ownership of products and a chance to work on AI and other new areas helps keep the best people engaged.

**For first-line managers**, the job is the most direct of all. They are the people who notice when someone is restless, who can open up a stretch assignment and who make the case for a promotion. Equipping them to do this well may be the single most effective retention move a centre can make.

### Collaboration across centres

Density brings one more benefit that centres often overlook. With so many GCCs in one city, there is a large community of leaders facing similar challenges. Peer forums, roundtables and shared learning between non-competing centres can help everyone raise the bar on leadership and talent, and strengthen Pune's position as a destination for global work.

The centres that gain most from these conversations are usually the ones that contribute most to them. Sharing what has worked in onboarding, manager development or AI adoption builds a centre's reputation in the local market, and that reputation is part of what attracts the next generation of leaders.
