Ask a GCC manager what makes their job hard and the answer is rarely technical. It is the global call where a decision was made without them. The request from HQ that arrived fully formed. The "yes" they gave on Tuesday that became a missed date on Friday.

Influence is the quiet skill behind every bigger mandate. Centres earn more ownership when their people are treated as peers by global stakeholders, and that depends less on what they know than on how they communicate, disagree and follow through.

## Why influence is hard from a captive centre

Most GCC managers work in a matrix. Their reporting line may be local, their priorities set by a product owner in another country, and their success judged by stakeholders they have rarely met in person. They have accountability without much formal authority.

Add time zones, cultural differences in how directness is read, and a history where the centre was set up to execute rather than decide, and it is easy to see why many capable managers default to compliance. Saying yes feels safer than saying "here is a better way".

The cost of that default is high. Every unchallenged unrealistic plan, every decision taken without India's input, reinforces the view that the centre follows rather than leads.

## The six habits of managers who get heard

Watch the managers HQ seeks out, and a consistent pattern appears. They are not always the most senior or the most technical. They have built a small set of habits.

### 1. Map the room before you enter it

Influential managers know who actually makes a decision, who shapes it and who can block it. They spend time understanding the informal network at HQ, not just the org chart. A short stakeholder map, updated every quarter, is one of the most useful tools a GCC manager can keep.

### 2. Learn what each stakeholder is measured on

People support proposals that help them succeed. A product director cares about release dates and customer impact. A finance partner cares about cost and predictability. Framing the same idea in each person's terms is not manipulation. It is respect for their priorities.

### 3. Put it in writing

Across time zones, the written word does most of the work. Managers who get heard send short, structured notes: the situation, the options, a recommendation and the decision needed. A good one-page note often achieves more than an hour-long call.

## Pushing back without damaging the relationship

The habit that separates peers from order-takers is constructive pushback. Many managers avoid it because they worry about appearing difficult, or because "no" carries more weight in some cultures than others.

The trick is to change what pushback looks like. Instead of "we cannot do this", the message becomes "here is what we can deliver by that date, here is what it would take to deliver everything, and here is what I recommend". Options turn a refusal into a decision the stakeholder owns.

Timing matters as much as wording. Pushback in the first conversation is a sign of judgement. The same concern raised two days before a deadline sounds like an excuse.

### 4. Run global meetings that decide things

Global meetings are expensive: several time zones, senior people, limited overlap. Managers who get heard treat them as decision points. They share a short agenda in advance, state the decision needed up front, and close with a written summary of who will do what by when.

### 5. Read across cultures

Directness, silence and disagreement are read differently in the US, the UK, Europe and across Asia. A comment that feels blunt in one context feels vague in another. The best managers learn the working style of their main counterparts and adjust, while staying clear about the substance.

### 6. Close the loop, visibly

Trust is built by doing what you said you would do, and by making sure people notice. A short follow-up after a commitment is met, or an early heads-up when it is at risk, does more for credibility than any presentation.

## Building the habits across a team

These habits are learnable, but they rarely improve through a single workshop. They improve with practice on real situations, feedback from the people on the other side, and a manager who models them.

A practical starting point for a centre:

1. **Pick one recurring global forum**, such as a weekly product sync, and agree a simple meeting standard: agenda, decision needed, written summary.
2. **Introduce a one-page decision note** for anything that needs HQ input, and coach managers on writing them.
3. **Ask HQ counterparts for feedback** on two or three behaviours, before and after a quarter of practice.
4. **Celebrate good pushback.** When a manager raises a concern early and offers options, recognise it publicly.

Influence compounds. Each well-run meeting, clear note and honest conversation makes the next one easier, and makes the case for giving the centre more to own.

### What leaders can do

Senior leaders in the centre have a part to play too. When they bring India managers into senior conversations, credit them by name in front of HQ and back their decisions publicly, they signal that influence from India is expected rather than unusual.
